How this map is made

Every plaque in London that has a position on it gets attributed to the stations nearest it, and the Underground is redrawn with people's names where the station names usually go. None of it is placed by hand. This page goes through it in the order a build does.

What you are looking at

London has been putting up commemorative plaques since 1866. Over 1,000 of them are English Heritage's blue plaques, and Open Plaques catalogues thousands more from every other scheme besides. The trouble is that they are scattered. You come across one by accident rather than setting out to see it, and there is no good way to look at more than a few at a time.

The Underground is the diagram Londoners already use to think about where things are, so the two get put together here. Each plaque is attributed to the stations nearest it, the network is redrawn as a tube map, and a themed map swaps each station's name for somebody commemorated near it.

1 · Fetch Open Plaques, TfL stations, TfL routes
2 · Version into SQLite, keeping every past state
3 · Derive plaque→station, geometry, themes
4 · Write five JSON files the page reads

The page itself is plain HTML with no build step. It loads the JSON and hands it to d3-tube-map, which draws diagrams in the Underground's own style.

Where the data comes from

Three feeds, none of them typed in by hand:

  • Open Plaques, whose London dump is a CSV republished as plaques are added. It holds 3,781 plaques, of which 3,675 carry coordinates. The rest have never been positioned, so there is nothing to attribute them to. The data is public domain under PDDL 1.0.
  • TfL station data, a zip of CSVs giving station names and the coordinates every distance here is measured from.
  • TfL's Unified API, for the lines and their route sequences: which stations each line calls at, in running order. The track between them is drawn from that.

Two further lookups fill in what a plaque does not say about the person on it. Wikidata supplies occupations from a controlled vocabulary. Wikipedia supplies the categories an article sits in, the opening paragraphs explaining a calendar occasion, and the portrait and opening paragraph beside each name. The plaque already carries the subject's Wikipedia URL, so there is nothing to look up but the content: 2,047 of the 2,049 subjects the plaques name have an article behind them.

Wikipedia answers for the stations themselves too, which is what the panel shows when a station is standing for nobody but itself: its photograph and the opening paragraph of its own article. Finding that article is most of the work, because London's stations are filed under three different conventions — Oval tube station, Stratford station, Abbey Road DLR station — and the bare name is usually taken by the district instead. List of London Underground stations names the article for all 272 tube stations outright; the Overground, DLR and Elizabeth line stops are tried against each convention in turn, and the first name that both exists, reads as a station and is not one of Wikipedia's disambiguation pages wins. All 421 resolve, every one of them with a photograph.

Nothing is overwritten

The database keeps the full history of every row. A version records the load it first appeared in and the load that replaced it, so re-running the build against an unchanged feed writes nothing at all. When a station gets renamed or a plaque corrected, the old value stays behind rather than being erased. Each table has a current_ view of the rows that are live now, and that is what everything downstream reads.

The join between TfL's two feeds is not quite clean. The API calls Euston 940GZZLUEUS while the station files list it under its hub code, HUBEUS. Falling back to the parent station id is what resolves every stop on all 19 lines onto a row that actually has coordinates.

Which station a plaque belongs to

Every station is measured against every plaque, which comes to 421 by 3,675 great-circle distances, computed as a single matrix. Ranking is then a sort down each column, and the three nearest stations are kept for each plaque.

Anything more than a kilometre from the nearest station is dropped. A kilometre is roughly a twelve-minute walk and takes in 88% of the positioned plaques. The tail runs out to 37 km, where the Elizabeth line reaches Reading and there are no plaques for miles. What survives is 3,238 plaques, covering 295 of the 421 stations, at a median walk of 324 m.

Three stations get kept rather than one because the closest station is often not the one anybody would actually use, and because a themed map that wants a different name at every station needs somewhere to fall back to when its first choice is taken.

How the diagram is drawn

The lines, stations and stretches of track are live TfL data. The positions are not. The real Tube map is a copyrighted design, licensed on terms that forbid altering it, renaming stations included, which happens to be the entire point of this project. So the diagram is drawn from scratch instead.

The drawing library will not accept an arbitrary polyline. Every segment has to run along one of the eight compass points, and a change of direction needs its own node one step past the end of the run. The geometry is worked out in four passes.

  • Place. Project each station's coordinates, then pull it towards the centre by the square root of its distance out. Beck's map gives the centre most of the room and squeezes the ends of the lines in. Without that compression the grid would need something like 1,000 cells to keep Covent Garden and Leicester Square apart, and would be mostly empty. Stations then take an integer grid cell, busiest first, so interchanges land where they asked to and the quiet stops shuffle around them.
  • Route. Join each pair of stations with straight runs and corners, trying short sequences before long ones and preferring the shortest drawn line. That preference is what puts the diagonals in. A line leaves a station the way it arrived, so all the turning happens in between. Four corners is a full reversal, the worst any line asks for, and that is the Central doubling back round the Hainault loop.
  • Validate. The library's own geometry rules, reimplemented and run over every branch before the JSON is written, so a mistake fails the build rather than the browser.
  • Label. Measure each name into a rectangle and score it on what it would cover. Landing on another name costs more than crossing a line, since two names on top of each other are unreadable, where a name over a line is only untidy. The most crowded stations choose first, then the worst few are placed again against everything else.

The last build reported no overlapping labels and 56 sitting close to a line. That overlap count is exact. The line count is pessimistic, because it treats a line as filling every cell it crosses. Anywhere the automatic result can be beaten by eye, a small overrides file is applied last, so hand-tuning survives a rebuild.

Where two lines share the same track they have to be drawn side by side, offset along their own normal, but only by one line width. Push further and a line tears open at its junctions, because each branch is offset along a different bearing, and a gap reads as the line ending.

How the people are chosen

The themed maps come from a single markdown file, which is the only thing that has to be edited to add one. It has three levels: a group, the categories within it, and the rules deciding which plaques a category covers. A rule can be any of four things.

  • A role, matched against what the plaque itself records. This is free text, filled in for 3,186 plaques and running to 1,091 distinct spellings of a job, so matching folds case and turns curly apostrophes into straight ones.
  • A field and value: the plaque's subject type, sex, colour, area, series or erecting organisation. The list of allowed fields is fixed, so a typo gets rejected by name instead of quietly matching nothing.
  • An occupation from Wikidata, where each word sits at the top of a subclass tree rather than at a leaf. Asking for writer therefore also answers for poets, novelists and playwrights, and for whatever occupation gets coined next year.
  • A Wikipedia category, matched loosely so that one line covers a whole family of them. Loose matching is what makes these worth having, and now and then it reaches somebody it should not. An except: line says so, which beats giving up and listing people by hand.

A category is the union of its rules, less its exceptions, and takes only plaques whose subject is a man or a woman. That keeps out the many plaques commemorating churches, public houses and bomb sites, which have no business on a map about people. Every group also gets a combined map of everything under it, since several categories are thin on their own and good together. There are 35 maps in all: the eight groups, their categories, and five that stand alone.

One name per station, one station per name

Both directions matter. A station can only carry one name, and a person ought to turn up once rather than at every station they happened to live near. Pairings are taken nearest first, and a person whose closest station is already spoken for falls back to the second or third station kept for them. That fallback is worth real coverage: Music fills 131 stations, where only about 80 have a musician as their single nearest plaque.

Matching is keyed on the person rather than the plaque. Dickens has twenty plaques and Hendrix three, and reading those as different people would put the same name on three different stations.

How the calendar works

Twelve of the maps carry a date. Opening the page does not give you the plain tube map, it gives you whichever map is in season, already chosen. That choice is made by the build rather than in the browser, because the build knows how long its own answer has to last.

Each build asks three questions in order.

  • Is a dated occasion within the next seven days? If so, that map takes the week. It looks at this year's date, or next year's if the day has already gone, which is how a build at the end of December still finds 10 January.
  • Is it the month of a month-long occasion? LGBT+ History Month and Black History Month really do run all month, so they take theirs.
  • Otherwise, whose turn is it? Every remaining week goes to a map with no occasion of its own, picked by the week number, so every build in the same week agrees without anything being remembered between them. Maps covering fewer than 40 stations sit the rotation out. Law has fourteen, which makes a map that just looks broken.

Seven days is not a round number picked at random. It is how long a build's output stays up, since the site rebuilds weekly. Any shorter and a date could fall in a week no build was ever looking at, so the map would miss the day itself and then show it late.

The panel at the top of the map says which map you are looking at. For a dated one it carries the opening of the Wikipedia article explaining the occasion, fetched at build time and folded away to its title if you would rather just look at the map. Every other map in the menu can still be picked at any time. The calendar only decides which one opens.

The chosen map is written to a small file of its own. The full set of themed maps runs to about 1.5 MB, six times the map itself, and is only worth fetching once somebody opens the menu.

How it stays up to date

The site is published from the repository to GitHub Pages. A push deploys whatever is committed. A scheduled run every Monday at 04:00 UTC does the whole thing: refetch all three feeds, rebuild the map, the themed maps and the calendar's choice, commit the regenerated JSON, then deploy that. The same run can be triggered by hand.

Because the database is not committed, the workflow caches it between runs, and the version history rolls forward from week to week instead of starting again from nothing.

What this map is not

  • Not geography. Positions are compressed towards the centre and every station is snapped to a grid cell, so a station sits where the diagram wants it rather than where it is. Distances on the page mean nothing. The distances in metres, from plaque to station, are real.
  • Not TfL's map, and not endorsed by them. The network data is theirs, under the Open Government Licence. The drawing belongs to this project, for the licensing reasons above.
  • Not only English Heritage's blue plaques. Open Plaques catalogues every commemorative scheme, in every colour. A category can narrow to one of them, but by default they are all in.
  • Not a record of who lived where. It records who somebody thought worth a plaque, with all the biases that decision carries: heavily male, heavily central, heavily nineteenth and twentieth century. A station with no name on a themed map means nothing nearby matched, not that nobody was there.
  • Only as good as the catalogue's fields. Roles are free text and inconsistent, and the sex field records 95 men as object, which is why subject type gets preferred to it wherever a category needs to know.
  • Hiding a lot, deliberately. Around a busy interchange there may be dozens of matching plaques, and the map shows one. Clicking a station is how to see what sits behind the name.
